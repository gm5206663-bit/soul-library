# Chapter Two — First Crew

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter Two: DOMO TRANSIT (before 5000 BC)
KIND      : Eternal recruit, days old
AGE       : newborn on this mission; prior missions wiped
CLASS     : Order I Foundational, Nascent — drill stance seed
THREAT    : Ikaris's first drill
BOND      : Makkari's sign-name; Gilgamesh's table
```

---

Earth was a blue coin at the bottom of a long fall, and it grew.

Varun stood at the viewport with his palm flat on the glass the way he had stood since boarding — the Domo hummed through the deck, through his boots, through the bones of his feet, and the hum had become his favorite thing about being alive. It never stopped. It asked nothing. It only carried.

A vibration joined the hum. Not the ship. Faster. Lighter. Coming close and stopping just behind his shoulder.

He turned. Makkari stood there with her head tilted, studying his palm on the glass the way she had studied everything about him since the cradle bay — completely, and all at once, the way other people glanced.

*Feel that?* she signed — or rather, she touched two fingers to the deck, then to her own ribs, then raised her eyebrows at him. The ship's song, and where it lived.

"I feel it," Varun said. "It lives in my teeth."

Her grin went quick and bright. Then she touched two fingers to her own chest, tapped twice — *tap tap* — and pointed at him. Then she did it again, slower, watching to see if he understood.

It took him a breath. Then: "That is me? That is my — my sign?"

She nodded, pleased, and tapped her own chest once — herself — then his sternum twice, lightly, with two fingers. The taps landed over his heartbeat. *Tap tap. You.*

"Tap tap," Varun said solemnly. "I have been alive for days and already I have a name no one can say out loud."

Makkari's shoulders shook. She pointed at him, then at the far end of the corridor, then at her own eyes, then at him again — and was gone before he finished blinking, a bow-wave of displaced air rocking him on his heels.

It took him another breath to understand: a race.

He ran.

---

He lost. Of course he lost. He lost so completely that Makkari had time to return to the viewport, fetch a coil of spare cable from a wall locker, return to the finish, and be leaning against the corridor wall braiding the cable when he arrived, breathing hard, boots skidding on the deck.

She held up the braid. Then she held up one finger. Then she pointed at him and shook her head, mournful.

Varun bent double, hands on his knees, chest heaving — and laughed.

It came out of him like something breaking open. He had never laughed before. None of them had ever heard him laugh before. The sound went down the corridor and came back off the far wall, and Makkari stared at him for half a second — and then she was laughing too, silently, both hands pressed over her mouth, shoulders shaking, the braid forgotten.

"You — " Varun gasped, straightening, wiping his eyes. His eyes were wet. He stared at his own fingers, fascinated. "You ran there and back and did *rope work*. I want a rematch. I want — " he looked down the corridor, judging distance the way his hands judged weight, "— a head start of one entire day."

Makkari considered this with enormous gravity. Then she nodded, magnanimous, and held up one finger again: *one day*. Then she tapped her chest twice — herself the generous — and vanished down the corridor in a ripple of displaced air, leaving the braided cable behind on the deck.

Varun picked it up. It was, he had to admit, an excellent braid.

"You will never beat her," said a voice behind him, warm with amusement. "None of us have. Ikaris tried once, in the cradle bay, before you woke. She lapped him."

Varun turned, coiling the cable. Gilgamesh filled the corridor junction with his bulk and his easy stance and the smell of something impossibly good — bread, and oil, and heat. "Come," the big Eternal said. "You have been at that window since boarding. The planet will still be there after you eat. Food first. Pining after rocks later."

"I was not pining," Varun said, falling into step beside him. "I was listening."

"Same thing, with you." Gilgamesh clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to stagger a recruit — Varun staggered, caught himself, and the big man laughed. "Good knees. You will need them. Ikaris drills at second bell."

"Second bell" was a ship's measure Varun had learned that morning: the Domo rang its hours in deep soft tones, and the crew ordered their transit by them. "What," Varun asked carefully, "is a drill?"

Gilgamesh's grin showed teeth. "You will see. Eat first. Everything fights better fed."

---

The galley was the warmest room on the Domo, and the loudest, and within one meal Varun understood it was the true heart of the ship. Not the bridge where Ajak stood her watches, not the engine rooms where Phastos argued with his instruments, but here, where eleven places had become eleven settings without anyone making a speech about it.

His setting was beside Gilgamesh, across from Sersi, with Makkari's empty chair (she ate standing, moving, everywhere) and Kingo holding court at the far end.

"— and then," Kingo was saying, spinning a coin of solid light across his knuckles. "the calibrator simply *hangs* in the air, in front of everyone, and Phastos makes a sound like a stepped-on instrument, and our Eleventh here catches a falling shard of cradle-glass with his bare hand and bleeds all over his own lattice — " the coin vanished, "— on his first day. *Show-off.*"

"I bled," Varun said, with dignity, around a mouthful of bread. "Bleeding is not showing off. Ask anyone."

"Bleeding *prettily* is showing off." Kingo leaned forward, squinting at him. "Look at him. Have you all *looked* at him? The temples are going to love him. The temples are going to carve him on walls and I am going to be standing next to him in all of them, which is the important part."

"He has storm-sea eyes," Sprite announced from the corner, where she was braiding her hair and everyone else's business. "Silver-grey. Like weather. Ajak, tell him he has weather eyes."

Ajak, at the head of the table, looked up from her meal with the ghost of a smile. "He has his own eyes, Sprite. Let him keep them."

It was gently said, and the table laughed, and Varun ducked his head — his hair fell forward, long and black and badly in need of tying back, and Sersi, beside him, quietly pushed a spare cord across the table without a word. He tied his hair back with fingers that shook slightly less than they had that morning, and the small competence of it steadied him.

"So," Kingo said, "the recruit speaks. The recruit bleeds. The recruit ties knots. Tell us, recruit — what do you *do*? Besides fall slowly and catch badly."

Eleven faces — ten faces — turned to him. Varun looked down the table at all of them: Gilgamesh's encouraging bulk, Sersi's gentleness, Kingo's mischief, Sprite's sharp delight, Phastos's distracted curiosity, Thena's still attention, Makkari's bright vibration, Druig's cold distance at the far end, Ikaris's measuring stare, Ajak's commander's patience.

He thought about it honestly. Then: "I hold," he said. "That is what I was told. Gravity-anchor. Kinetic-shunt." A pause. "I do not know what the second word means yet. I am hoping no one asks me to spell it."

The table exploded. Gilgamesh's landslide laugh shook the settings. Kingo pointed at him, wheezing: "*Him.* I like him. He stays." Even Thena's mouth moved — not quite a smile, but the rumor of one. Makkari slapped the table twice in delight.

Varun sat in the middle of the laughter with bread in one hand and the braided cable — Makkari's trophy, which he had carried in like a fool — still looped over his wrist, and felt something settle in his chest that had been unmoored since the cradle: not a designation. A seat. He had a seat at a table, and people who laughed when he spoke, and the difference between the two was the difference between being made and belonging.

Across the table, Ajak watched him laugh — watched the new recruit discover mirth the way he had discovered gravity, by falling into it — and something in her face softened past command into something older and more tired and more tender. Then Ikaris spoke from down the table, and the moment passed.

"Second bell," Ikaris said. "Recruit. The drill deck."

---

The drill deck was a wide bare circle of deck plating under high cold lights, ringed by the ship's bones. It smelled of ozone and effort. Weapons that were part of Thena leaned in a rack that was also part of the ship. At the circle's edge stood a rack of practice staves, blunt and scarred.

Ikaris stood at the circle's center, stripped to shirtsleeves, calm as drawn stone. "The law you were given," he said without preamble, "is restraint. The skill you will learn here is its opposite: when restraint ends, you end things." He gestured Varun into the circle. "Stance first. Feet. Show me how you stand when the world tries to move you."

Varun stepped in. Found the stance his body had found in the cradle bay — one foot forward, one back, weight sunk low. The stance of someone who intended to hold.

Ikaris circled him once. "Where did you learn that?"

"I did not," Varun said. "My knees knew."

A flicker crossed Ikaris's face — interest, or suspicion, filed for later. "Hold it. When I come at you, do not run. Do not dodge. *Hold.*"

He came.

Later, Varun would learn that Ikaris had come at quarter strength, at half speed, with every lethal instinct leashed — a kindness, of its kind. At the time, it felt like being hit by weather. The rush crossed the circle faster than his eyes could follow; something struck his guard like a falling wall; the deck slammed into his back; the cold lights wheeled overhead.

"Up," Ikaris said.

Varun got up. Found the stance. Breathed the way Ajak had taught him in the cradle bay — in, out, slower.

Ikaris came again. This time Varun *felt* it coming — not with his eyes, which were useless, but with his feet, with the deck, with the lean of the air. And his hands shaped themselves around absent weight, and the rush struck his crossed forearms instead of his chest, and he slid three full strides with his boots smoking on the plating and *stayed upright*.

"Better," Ikaris said. "Again."

The third rush broke his guard and folded him. The fourth put him on the deck hard enough to drive the breath out in a rush. The fifth —

The fifth, something in him *refused*. Not his mind. His hands. His feet. The same unbelonging memory that had shaped his fingers since waking rose up through the new bruises and the ringing ears, and when Ikaris came he did not brace. He *gave*, the way water gives, the way Makkari's corridor had given under his running feet — and caught the rush in crossed arms and *turned* with it, and for one impossible heartbeat the unstoppable second-in-command of the Earth mission staggered sideways, off-balance, boots scraping for purchase —

— and Varun went down anyway, because catching Ikaris was like catching a thrown mountain, and the mountain did not care that you had good form.

Silence in the drill circle. Varun lay on his back, staring at the lights, every bone ringing, his forearms one long bruise, his lattice flickering gold-white-gold across the backs of his hands like a guttering lamp.

Boots. Ikaris stood over him, looking down — and the measuring stare had changed. Not warm. Ikaris was never warm. But *revised*.

"Up," Ikaris said, and this time it was almost gentle. "Again."

From the circle's edge, Thena's voice cut clean as her spear-light: "Feet." Varun looked — she stood with her arms folded, watching with total attention. "Your feet were right. Your shoulders argued with them. Stop arguing." She turned to Ikaris. "He holds. Teach him to hold *longer*."

And from the shadows beyond the lights, where he had watched the whole drill without stepping into it, Druig spoke for the first time: "Careful, brother. The ones who hold the longest crack the loudest." A pause. "Just an observation."

Ikaris did not look at him. "Again, recruit."

They went again. And again. By the end of it Varun could barely lift his arms, his stance held on memory alone, and when Ikaris finally called the halt, the second-in-command did something no one on the watching edge had expected: he offered his hand, hauled Varun upright, and held the grip a beat.

"You hold," Ikaris said. Simply. A fact, filed. "Good. Tomorrow, longer."

It was, Varun would learn, the highest praise Ikaris had. He carried it to the medbay like a medal, walking on shaking legs, grinning through a split lip.

---

The medbay was quiet after the drill deck — low light, clean air, the ship's hum softened to a murmur. Ajak's hands were warm on his forearms, and where they passed, the deep bruising loosened its grip, the lattice steadying from guttering flicker back to its slow breathing rhythm.

"Tell me about the flinch," Ajak said.

Varun blinked. "The — ?"

"At the start of the drill. When Ikaris said *hold the line*." Her thumbs pressed gently along a bruise, watching his face, not her work. "Your shoulders went up. Your jaw set. For half a breath, recruit, you looked like a wild thing deciding whether the fence was worth the field." She smiled, a commander's smile with something underneath it. "I have commanded soldiers for longer than this ship has flown. I know a freedom-flinch when I see one."

Varun was quiet. The medbay hummed. Somewhere beyond the walls, Makkari's vibration passed like weather, and was gone.

"I do not know why the orders chafe," he said at last, honestly. "I am days old. I have obeyed nothing yet except breathing. But when he said it — *hold the line* — something in me said *whose line?* And then I was ashamed of asking." He looked at her — Prime Eternal, healer, the one who had named him out of depth and holding. "Is it wrong? To ask?"

Ajak's hands stilled on his arms. For a long moment she said nothing, and in the quiet Varun saw what command cost her. Saw it the way he had seen it in the cradle bay, in the voice that did not change on the words *we watch*: the knowing, and the carrying, and the truth behind the truth, held alone because no one else on the ship could hold it yet.

"No," she said softly. "It is not wrong to ask. It is the asking that makes you worth commanding." She resumed her work, thumbs easing the last of the bruising. "But hear me, Varun. You were made to hold — and holding means feeling every weight. Every order you obey against your grain. Every field you stand in while the fence holds. It will cost you. Year upon year upon year, it will cost you." Her eyes met his — steady, grieved, unflinching. "And I am sorry. Before any of it. I am sorry."

He held her gaze — silver-grey on dark, recruit on Prime — and nodded. It was not understanding; he was days old, and understanding would take centuries. It was acceptance. It was the stance, found again: one foot forward, one back, weight sunk low.

"Then I will hold," he said. "And you will tell me when the fence is wrong. Promise me that much."

Something moved in her face — surprise, or pain, or the beginning of a grief years from fruition. "I promise," Ajak said.

---

Earth filled more of the glass than it had that morning. Blue deepened into blue. White curled and tore. Somewhere under those clouds, things with teeth were hunting things with hands, and the Domo fell toward them with its eleven lights and its orders and its galley laughter and its drill-deck bruises.

Varun stood at the viewport with his palm on the glass, hair tied back with Sersi's cord, forearms aching pleasantly under fresh-healed skin, Makkari's braided cable still looped over his wrist like a trophy he had no intention of surrendering.

A vibration joined the hum. Makkari arrived beside him without hurry, for once — no race, no game — and stood looking at the blue coin with him. After a moment, she lifted her own hand, and set her palm on the glass beside his.

Two palms. One glass. The whole fall ahead of them.

*Tap tap*, she signed against the viewport frame, softly enough to feel and no more. *You. Here.*

"Here," Varun agreed.

The Domo fell toward morning, and this time he fell with a crew.

*End of Chapter Two.*
