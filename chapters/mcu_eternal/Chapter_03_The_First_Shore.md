# Chapter Three: The First Shore

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter Three: ARRIVAL c. 5000 BC · Mesopotamia (Eridu-phase coast)
POV: Varun · CAST: all eleven Eternals · NEW: none (no new mechanics)
BOND: Makkari↗ · Ikaris↗ · Gilgamesh↗ · Ajak= · Thena= · Druig=
POWER: HOLD-BUBBLE (ramp shelter) / ANCHOR-FEET (stand, three breaths) / CATCH on self (fall, uncontrolled)
SEED: shell-bead, second of two (Varun's first gift received) · NEXT: the attack order
```

---

Earth filled the viewport like a held breath about to break.

Varun stood with his forehead nearly touching the glass, watching blue and brown and white turn beneath the ship, and felt the strangest pressure behind his ribs — as if his chest were a small room and something larger than the room was trying to stand up inside it.

"Big," he said. It was not his best line. It was the only one he had.

Beside him, Makkari’s hands moved in a flicker too fast to follow — then slowed, deliberately, when she saw his face. She signed it again, patient as a teacher with a slow student. *Land. Run. You. Me.*

"The rematch," Varun said. "Already calling it. We have not even landed and you are calling it."

*Scared?*

"Terrified. My knees are shaking. Look at them. No, do not look at them, that was a joke, do not—"

Her grin flashed white. *Land. Then run. Then you lose.*

"Then I lose gracefully," Varun said, "which is a kind of winning, and I will explain that to you at length while you wait for me at the finish."

Behind them the bridge hummed with arrival-work — Phastos calling numbers to no one, Kingo narrating his own readiness to Sprite, who was narrating it back in a merciless impression of him. Ikaris stood at the forward rail with his hands clasped behind his back, and the set of his shoulders said that this — this exact moment, this descent — was the thing he had been built standing toward.

Thena passed behind Varun on her way to the rail. She did not slow. She tapped the toe of his boot once with hers as she went by.

"Feet," she said.

Varun looked down at his feet, planted wide on the humming deck. He adjusted them — half a step, weight low, the stance from the sparring circle.

"Yes, ma’am," he said to her back.

Makkari’s shoulders shook silently. *Teacher’s favorite,* she signed.

"I am everyone’s favorite," Varun said. "It is a burden. Ask anyone."

---

The ship came down through cloud like a needle through cloth, and the coast rose to meet it — a low green-brown seam between grey water and paler sky, stitched with the dark thread of a river. Reed beds. Mud flats shining like hammered bronze. A scatter of flat-roofed huts, small as dice from this height, and thin threads of smoke rising perfectly straight in the still air.

People, down there. Small moving dots. Staring up.

Varun’s throat went dry.

"They see us," he said quietly.

"Of course they see us," Druig said from the rail, where he leaned with his arms crossed and his knives of cheekbones catching the viewport light. "We are a golden mountain falling out of the sky. Subtlety was never on the manifest."

"I meant—" Varun started. "They have never seen anything like us. Like this. They will remember this day for the rest of their lives, and tell their children, and their children will tell—" He stopped. The pressure behind his ribs again, standing up. "We are about to become someone’s oldest story."

Druig looked at him for a long moment. Something crossed his face — surprise, or the ghost of it, quickly buried.

"Do not let it go to your head, gravity boy," he said. "Stories end."

The deck shuddered. Somewhere deep in the ship, landing struts unfolded with a sound like a giant clearing its throat. The viewport filled with mud and reeds and running, pointing, scattering people — and then the ship settled, and the engines sighed into silence, and for one whole breath nobody on the bridge said anything at all.

Then Ajak’s voice came over the ship, calm as deep water.

"All of us. The ramp. Now."

---

The ramp went down with the slow majesty of a drawn sword, and the smell of Earth came up it like a wall — mud and salt and smoke and rot and green growing things and fish, all of it raw and rotten and alive at once. Varun stood halfway down with his mouth open, breathing it in like soup.

"It stinks," Kingo announced from the top of the ramp, with deep satisfaction. "Magnificently. I love it here."

"It smells like the beginning of everything," Sersi said softly, and Sprite, beside her, wrinkled her nose and said, "It smells like wet dog," and Sersi laughed — actually laughed, bright as a bell — and the sound of it seemed to startle even her.

Varun reached the bottom of the ramp and his boots sank into the mud up to the ankle, and the mud was cold, and the cold came straight up through the soles and into his bones, and he laughed out loud because it was the first cold thing he had ever stood in and it was wonderful.

Then he looked up, and the laughter stopped in his chest.

They were all around — dozens of them, men and women and children, pressed back against the huts in a wide frightened ring, staring. Their faces were brown from the sun and thin from short food. They wore undyed wool and woven reed and leather. Some held clay sickles, harvesting blades, the way people hold weapons when they have never held a weapon. A child no older than his own first hour of life — no older than anything — clung to her mother’s leg and stared at Varun with her whole face.

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed. The smoke went up straight.

Varun did the only thing he could think of. He waved.

The child hid her face. Someone in the crowd made a sound like a sob cut in half. And Varun stood there with his hand in the air and the mud freezing his feet and thought, with sudden terrible clarity: *we are giants to them. We are monsters who fell from the sky. Nothing I do in the next ten breaths will matter as much as what I do right now.*

He lowered his hand slowly. He smiled — small, careful, the least frightening smile he owned. He pointed at himself.

"Varun," he said. Then he pointed at the ground, at all of them, at the whole wide muddy living world. "Friend. We— friend."

The child peeked out from behind her mother’s leg.

That was when the screaming started.

---

It came from the water — from the shallows off the reed beds, where a reed boat had overturned and a man was waist-deep in grey surf with a child held above his head, and something was rising out of the water behind him.

Varun had seen pictures of Deviants. Pictures were nothing. This was a wall of wet grey muscle and teeth, hound-shaped and horse-sized, water sheeting off its flanks, its jaw already open — and the man saw its shadow fall over him and screamed his son’s name at the sky.

What happened next happened all at once, and Varun would remember it for the rest of his life as a series of still pictures with no air between them.

Ikaris, launching off the ramp like a spear thrown at heaven, trailing light.

The winged one dropping out of the sun with its talons spread — the second Deviant, the one nobody had seen — and Ikaris meeting it in midair above the screaming man, one hand closing around its throat, the heat-vision blooming white-gold, the thing coming apart in fire and falling into the surf in burning pieces.

The child, snatched out of the falling water by a streak of gold and dust — Makkari, there and gone before the splash landed, setting the boy down on dry sand a hundred paces away and signing *stay* at him so fiercely the boy sat down hard and stayed.

The rest of the pack coming out of the reeds — four, five, six of them, low hound-shapes with teeth like broken glass — and the team scattering to meet them the way eleven strangers scatter, each alone, each superb, each fighting a private war.

Thena’s blades sang. Kingo’s hands bloomed light. Gilgamesh met the biggest hound head-on with a laugh like a landslide and fists like the end of the argument. Phastos’s constructs unfolded mid-sprint. Sersi transmuted a charging beast’s forelegs to marsh-mud and watched it face-plant with an expression of mild apology. Sprite was suddenly twelve Sprites, all of them rude. Druig stood over the villagers’ huddle with his arms spread, shouting them down into stillness before their panic could scatter them into the killing ground.

"Down! Stay down! Eyes on me, all of you, eyes on ME—"

And Ajak — Ajak was already moving toward the wounded fisherman in the surf, already glowing gold at the hands, already exactly where she needed to be, because of course she was.

Which left the ramp. Which left Varun.

And the gap.

---

He saw it the way you see a leak in a hull — one hound, smaller and faster than the rest, slipping the edge of the fight where Kingo’s blasts had driven the pack sideways, slipping past Phastos’s half-built barrier, slipping through with its belly in the mud and its eyes fixed on the villagers’ huddle crouched behind the ramp.

On the children. On the girl who had peeked out at him.

Varun moved.

He did not fly — he could not fly. He ran, boots tearing out of the mud, and planted himself in the gap between the hound and the huddle with his feet wide and his knees bent and his palms out, and the stance was Thena’s stance, the one from the circle, and he heard her voice in his head like a bell. *Feet.*

"You," he told the hound, "shall not. Pass."

The hound came on, jaws wide, and Varun threw both palms forward and *held*.

The bubble went up around the huddle — thin, shimmering, barely there, a soap-film of bent gravity stretched over screaming mothers and crying children — and the hound hit it at full sprint and bounced.

It rolled, snarling, came up furious, and hit it again. Claws raked the film. Teeth closed on nothing. The bubble dented inward, toward the children, and Varun felt the weight of the beast land on his shoulders like a falling roof and *held* — knees shaking, teeth bared, gold starting from his nose in a hot thread.

One breath. The hound circled, looking for an edge. There was no edge. There was only Varun, and the weight, and the screaming behind him going suddenly quiet as the villagers realized the teeth could not reach them.

Two breaths. The hound charged the film again and the impact drove Varun half a step back, boots plowing mud, and something hot burst in his ears, and the world narrowed to the shimmer and the teeth and the weight.

Three breaths. The hound gathered itself, hackles up, and hit the bubble with everything it had — and the bubble held, and Varun did not.

His knees went. The sky tilted. He fell flat on his back in the freezing mud with the weight still on his chest and his palms still up, and through the shimmer he saw the hound rise over him, jaws opening wide enough to take his whole head—

—and Gilgamesh’s fist came down on it like the judgment of mountains.

Once. The hound went into the mud and did not get up.

Silence, except for the surf, and the ringing in Varun’s ears, and his own breath tearing in and out of his chest.

Gilgamesh looked down at him. The big man’s knuckles were split and gold-seeping. His grin was enormous.

"Good wall," he said.

Varun stared up at the sky. The sky was grey and huge and indifferent and beautiful. "Walls fall," he managed.

"This one stood," Gilgamesh said, and reached down a hand the size of a ham, and hauled Varun upright like a sack of grain. "Three breaths. I counted. Good wall."

---

The battle was over. It had lasted less time than breakfast.

Varun stood swaying in the mud with gold drying on his upper lip, and watched his crew — his strange, superb, solitary crew — stand down from their private wars. Kingo extinguished his hands with a flourish and a bow to no one. Thena cleaned her blades on the wind. Sersi was already kneeling in the surf beside the wounded fisherman, murmuring, her hands glowing green-gold as she knit closed what the teeth had opened. Makkari reappeared at Varun’s elbow out of nowhere, took one look at his face, and signed, *Stupid. Brave. Stupid-brave.*

"Thank you," Varun said. "I accept all three."

*Rematch postponed,* she signed, with great dignity. *You would lose too easily right now. It would embarrass us both.*

"I appreciate your mercy," Varun said solemnly. "It is the only mercy I have received all day."

Then Ajak was there — suddenly, the way she was always suddenly there — with gold on her hands and command in her eyes, tilting his chin up to look at the blood. Her fingers were warm. Her eyes were not.

"Report," she said. "Injuries."

"Nose. Ears. Pride. In that order." Varun tried to grin. It came out crooked. "The bubble held. I did not. Design flaw. I will file a complaint with whoever built me."

"The bubble held," Ajak repeated. She wiped the gold from his lip with her thumb, efficient as a surgeon, and the glow sank into his skin and the ringing in his ears faded to a hum. "Three breaths against a full charge. On your first deployment. With an ability you have held for a matter of weeks." She stepped back. Her gaze went past him, to the huddle — the mothers, the children, the girl who had peeked, all of them alive, all of them staring. "You held the ramp, Varun. As ordered."

"As ordered," Varun echoed. He looked down at his hands. They were shaking. They were covered in mud and his own gold blood. Behind him, the villagers were beginning to make sounds — not screaming anymore. Something else. Something like singing, or praying, and he did not know which frightened him more.

He knelt in the mud. With one shaking, bloodied finger, he drew a line in the wet sand in front of the huddle — a long straight line, the length of a man lying down.

Ajak watched him. "What is that?"

Varun looked up at her. "The law," he said. "From today. Behind this line — they live. Whatever comes out of the water, whatever falls out of the sky, whatever the cost. Behind this line, they live." He wiped his finger on his suit and left a gold smear across the deep blue. "That is the whole of it. Everything else is details."

Ajak was quiet for a long moment. The surf hissed. The smoke went up straight. Somewhere behind them, Sersi laughed softly at something the fisherman’s son said.

"The law is bigger than one beach, Varun," Ajak said at last. Not unkindly. The way a commander speaks to a soldier who has just learned the first word of a long language.

"Then it starts here," Varun said.

She looked at him — really looked, the healer’s gaze and the commander’s gaze at once — and nodded once, and moved on to the next wounded thing that needed her. Which was her whole answer. Which was, Varun was learning, the way she gave all her important answers.

---

He was sitting on an overturned reed basket with a wad of wool pressed to his nose, feeling profoundly sorry for himself in the way only the very young and the barely-alive can, when he saw Sersi do it.

The fisherman’s son — the boy Makkari had carried out of the surf — was standing in front of her, dry now, wrapped in someone’s cloak, staring up at her with eyes like twin moons. And Sersi knelt, so that her eyes were level with his, and took a small knife from her belt — a good knife, bronze-bright, practical — and pressed it into his hands, folding his fingers around the hilt.

A tool. Not a weapon. The distinction was the whole gift.

The boy stared at the knife. Then at her. Then he said something in his own tongue — quick, breathless, shining — and Sersi smiled, and ruffled his hair, and stood, and that was all. No speech. No ceremony. The sea hissed and the gulls screamed and the world changed shape around one small bright thing given freely.

Varun watched, and forgot to feel sorry for himself, and the wad of wool slipped from his nose into the mud.

Then a shadow fell over him, small and hesitant, and he looked up.

It was the girl. The one who had peeked. She stood in front of his basket with her hands clenched behind her back and her mother hovering three paces behind her, wringing wool between her fingers, and the girl stared at Varun. At the gold drying on his lip, at the mud caked to his knees, at the eyes that he knew were the wrong color for any sky she had ever seen — and then, quickly, before her courage could fail, she thrust out one fist and opened it.

A bead. A shell-bead, pink-white, threaded on a frayed strand of wool. Small. Chipped. Worth nothing to anyone in the universe except the girl who had carried it, and the boy-shaped god she was giving it to.

"For—" she said, in her own tongue, and then, carefully, in his: "Friend."

Varun stared at the bead in her palm. His throat closed. The pressure behind his ribs stood up so suddenly he thought his chest would crack open.

He took the bead with fingers that shook worse than they had under the hound’s weight, and then — because something in him demanded symmetry, demanded honesty — he reached into the seam of his suit where he had kept it since the first day of his life, and drew out the other one. The temple bead. The first bright thing anyone had ever given him.

He held them both in his palm, side by side. Cheap. Bright. His whole fortune.

"Friend," he said to the girl, and his voice came out rough as gravel, and he did not care. "Always."

Her smile went across her face like sunrise.

---

Ikaris called the formation at sunset.

They stood in a line on the sand with the ship rising behind them and the sea hissing in front — eleven against the grey-gold sky, battered, muddy, gold-seamed, unbeaten. And the villagers gathered at a careful distance and stared, and the children pointed, and nobody screamed, and that, Varun thought, limping into his place at the end of the line with Gilgamesh’s steadying hand under his elbow, that was the real victory. Not the dead beasts cooling in the surf. This. The absence of screaming.

Makkari, on his other side, jogged his elbow. He looked down. She signed, slow so he would not miss it: *Good wall.*

"You heard that?" Varun whispered. "You were a hundred paces away."

*I hear everything,* she signed, serene. *I am very fast. Sound cannot escape me.*

"That is not how sound works."

*That is how I work,* she signed, and faced forward, chin up, golden in the sunset, and Varun faced forward too, because Ikaris was raising his hand and the line was straightening and the story was being written right now, on this sand, in front of these witnesses, and he wanted his part of it to be upright.

His fist closed around the two beads in his pocket. Shell and clay. Pink-white and temple-red. The first thing given, and the second.

Cheap, he thought, watching the sun go down on the first shore of the rest of his life. Bright.

And he stood straight, and did not fall, and the line held.
